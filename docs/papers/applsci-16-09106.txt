Blockchain architectures increasingly rely on smart contracts as programmable execution
components, yet the security challenges they introduce remain only partially addressed.
Fuzz testing has emerged as one of the leading automated techniques for smart contract
vulnerability discovery; however, a systematic treatment linking classical fuzzing concepts
to the specific architectural constraints of smart contract execution environments has remained elusive. We conduct a large-scale systematic review of 258 publications collected
from Scopus and Google Scholar. The review is structured around four research questions
covering general fuzzing limitations, EVM execution constraints, cross-contract interaction
challenges, and technique transferability. The contributions are fourfold. First, we develop
a conceptual taxonomy establishing an explicit correspondence between classical and smart
contract fuzzing. Second, we present an architectural taxonomy of representative fuzzers
unified under a generalised waypoint architecture that exposes key feedback domains and
composition gaps. Third, we provide a categorised technique review spanning coverageguided, hybrid, and learning-based approaches. Fourth, we offer a reproducibility critique
with a structured research roadmap. Coverage-guided greybox fuzzing emerges as the
dominant paradigm (51.2% of the 121 SC fuzzer tools); learning-based approaches (machine learning, reinforcement learning, and LLMs) are a small but emerging class, with
LLM-guided fuzzing the fastest-emerging subcategory by recency. Feedback-domain composition remains sparse: most surveyed fuzzers combine only a few of the eight identified
feedback domains, and none approaches the full set, leaving several high-value multidomain compositions unexplored. Open challenges include scalable stateful exploration,
standardised benchmarks and oracles, and EVM-specific architectural optimisations. The
complete categorised corpus, coding, and search strategy are openly available.

1. Introduction
Blockchain technology, originally conceived as a mechanism for maintaining tamperresistant, chronologically ordered records across a distributed network, has matured from
early cryptocurrency experiments into a general-purpose infrastructure for decentralised
applications. Bitcoin [1] demonstrated the viability of a peer-to-peer electronic cash system
in 2008, and Ethereum [2] subsequently broadened the paradigm by introducing smart
contracts: self-executing programs deployed on-chain that run deterministically in the
Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). The concept of encoding contractual obligations in
executable code dates back to Szabo [3], but it was Ethereum’s Turing-complete execution
environment that made programmable, composable financial logic a practical reality. A
deployed Ethereum smart contract is identified by an address, may hold a balance, and
exposes a set of callable functions with which external actors interact by submitting transactions that invoke these functions. The predominant development language is Solidity [4],
although alternatives such as Vyper and Yul are also in use.
Unfortunately, the adoption of blockchain and smart contracts has also been accompanied by severe and costly attacks, often due to domain-specific security pitfalls in smart
contract implementations. The 2016 DAO exploit drained approximately $60 million in
Ether by exploiting a reentrancy vulnerability; the 2017 Parity Multisig wallet bugs led to
the permanent freezing of approximately $150 million; and the DeFi ecosystem has suffered
substantial cumulative losses from smart contract exploits [5]. As of 2025, over 60 million
smart contracts have been deployed on Ethereum, collectively managing over $100 billion
in total value locked (TVL) in DeFi protocols alone. The economic stakes make automated
vulnerability discovery not merely desirable but essential: a single undetected bug in a
widely used DeFi protocol can cause losses in the hundreds of millions.
Recognising this risk, the research community, industry, and non-profit organisations have invested heavily in techniques for verifying and testing smart contract
correctness [5–7]. These span static analysis (e.g., Slither [8]), which inspects the source
or bytecode for known vulnerability patterns without executing the contract; symbolic
and concolic execution (e.g., Oyente [9], Mythril [10]), which reason over path constraints
to reach specific program states; and formal verification, which establishes correctness
guarantees against a specification. Each technique occupies a different point in the trade-off
between coverage, precision, and soundness, and they are increasingly used in combination
rather than in isolation. Among these, fuzz testing (commonly known as fuzzing) has
emerged as one of the most practical and effective automated approaches for vulnerability discovery. Originally introduced by Miller et al. [11] as a random input generation
aimed at triggering anomalous program behaviours, fuzzing has evolved dramatically over
three decades: modern fuzzers integrate coverage-guided greybox mutation (AFL [12],
libFuzzer [13]), symbolic execution, machine learning (ML), and large language models
(LLMs) to improve effectiveness. Nevertheless, applying fuzzing to smart contracts introduces challenges that have no direct parallel in classical software testing. These include the
stateful, transactional execution model of the EVM; the complexity of multi-contract interactions; domain-specific vulnerability classes (reentrancy, integer overflow, access-control
violations); and the absence of standardised benchmarks and oracles. Figure 1 sketches out
this smart contract fuzzing loop at a high level, before the formal treatment in Section 3
is conducted. While the literature on smart contract analysis has grown substantially, a
systematic treatment that links classical fuzzing theory to these domain-specific challenges,
and organises the resulting body of work around a coherent formal and architectural
framework, has remained elusive.

This survey addresses this gap. The aim of this article is to conduct a systematic
review of the smart contract fuzzing literature, providing a structured analysis of methods,
techniques, and architectural designs under a coherent formal and taxonomic framework.
The review is structured following the PRISMA 2020 framework [14] and addresses four
research questions covering general fuzzing limitations, domain-level smart contract challenges, EVM-specific constraints, and technique transferability. The remainder of this
article is organised as follows: Section 2 presents the review methodology, including the
research questions, search strategy, study selection, and related works. Section 3 develops
the taxonomy linking classical to smart contract fuzzing with formal models. Section 4
surveys smart contract fuzzer architectures. Section 5 reviews fuzzing techniques across six
categories. Section 6 discusses the results, evaluation methodology, open challenges, and
lessons learned. Section 7 concludes the survey.

2. Methodology
Systematic reviews aim to generate consensus and address specific problems, establishing a solid foundation for research [15,16]. This survey provides a structured analysis
and classification of fuzzing approaches for Ethereum and EVM-compatible smart contracts, covering methods, techniques, and architectural designs. We examine the limitations
and challenges documented in both the general fuzzing literature and the smart contractspecific literature, with particular attention paid to techniques that may transfer between
the two domains. The scope is restricted to fuzz testing; other blockchain concerns (consensus mechanisms, network-layer scalability) are excluded unless they directly influence the
fuzzing process. For this reason, the systematic review was conducted under the PRISMA
2020 framework, which provides a checklist ensuring transparency and reproducibility.
Section 2.3 positions our work against prior surveys. As the first step in the methodology,
the following research questions, focused on smart contract fuzzing, were established:
•
•

•

•

RQ1 (General fuzzing): What are the current general limitations and challenges in
fuzz testing?
RQ2 (Domain-level): What limitations currently exist when fuzz testing smart contracts in general, such as challenges arising from the transactional execution model,
stateful semantics, and domain-specific vulnerability classes that distinguish smart
contracts from traditional software?
RQ3 (Platform-level): Within Ethereum and EVM-compatible smart contracts specifically, what additional limitations do existing fuzzing techniques face due to EVM
semantics (gas model, storage layout, cross-contract calls), the Solidity language, and
the on-chain deployment environment?
RQ4 (Transfer): What effective fuzzing techniques commonly employed in broader
software testing are not yet widely used in the context of smart contracts and
vice versa?

RQ2 and RQ3 are intentionally separated: RQ2 captures platform-agnostic smart
contract challenges (e.g., transaction-sequence generation, oracle design for reentrancy or

2.1. Search Strategy
To collect the relevant literature, we performed searches in two complementary
databases: Scopus and Google Scholar. Using two databases with different indexing
scopes follows the established systematic literature review (SLR) best practice and helps
mitigate single-source bias. We first performed a preliminary reading to identify domain
keywords (e.g., smart contract, Solidity, EVM) and fuzzing-related terms (e.g., fuzzing, fuzz
testing, coverage-guided, property-based).
For Scopus, we used field-restricted queries (TITLE-ABS-KEY) and configured savedsearch alerts covering smart contract fuzzing, classical fuzzing surveys, and their intersection, plus expanded queries for method- and vulnerability-focused tracking. For Google
Scholar, we adapted the same conceptual queries using the allintitle operator. Scopus
yielded 647 raw records and Google Scholar yielded 504 raw records (1151 combined). After
removing 463 duplicates, 688 unique items remained. The saved-search alerts were monitored from early 2025, with 15 February 2026 taken as the final search date; no publicationyear restriction was applied, so the corpus spanned the literature up to that date.
The exact query strings are reproduced in Supplementary S1. For Scopus we ran three
field-restricted TITLE-ABS-KEY queries (S1 smart contract fuzzing; S2 fuzzing surveys;
S3 their intersection) and three expanded queries (SA core-broad, SB methods/advanced,
SC LLM/ML-assisted) to improve recall for specific method families. For Google Scholar
we adapted the same concepts with the allintitle operator (G1–G3 baseline; GA domain,
GB method-family, GC ML/LLM). Two databases were used deliberately rather than
exhaustively: Scopus indexes the IEEE, ACM, Springer, and Elsevier venues in which
this literature predominantly appears, so a separate pass over IEEE Xplore, the ACM
Digital Library, or Web of Science would largely re-cover the same peer-reviewed records;
Google Scholar complements Scopus by adding grey literature and preprints (e.g., arXiv)
that Scopus does not index. Using one comprehensive index plus one broad grey-literature
engine follows established dual-source SLR practice and mitigates single-source indexing
bias. We note the reproducibility limits of the Google Scholar component: the allintitle
operator restricts matching to title terms and therefore omits in-scope works whose titles
do not contain the query terms; searches are run logged-out, and Scholar’s ranking, result
count, and localisation are not deterministic across sessions, so exact result sets may not
be reproducible. The Scopus baseline queries S1–S3 are reproducible from the strings
provided; SA–SC are broader recall-oriented expansions of S1 (core-broad, method-family,
and ML/LLM term variants) described in Supplementary S1, and all results returned
by each Scholar query are screened, with the raw exports released so the record set is
reproducible even where the live query is not.

2.2. Study Selection and Data Extraction
Application of the inclusion/exclusion criteria (IC1–IC3, EC1–EC3) to the 688 unique
records yielded 258 included publications. The 430 excluded items were predominantly
smart contract vulnerability-detection studies that did not employ fuzzing (EC2). Of
the 258 included items, 230 originated from Scopus and 28 from Google Scholar. Each
item included was assigned to exactly one of five categories: smart contract fuzzer tools
(n = 121); web3 fuzzing surveys (n = 31); classical fuzzing surveys (n = 96); classical
fuzzing techniques/tools (n = 7); and other (n = 3).
We distinguish two populations that are never conflated. The umbrella corpus of
258 publications records the full breadth of the PRISMA screening and is used only for the
flow diagram and corpus-size statements; it is never used as a denominator. All analytical
rates reported in this survey—technique shares, platform distribution, oracle usage, venue
tiers, and publication-year growth—are computed on the fuzzer core of 121 smart contract
fuzzer tools (118 unique) that satisfy the operational fuzzing-loop rule. Within this core,
nested sub-samples support the architectural analysis: 33 representative tools, of which
29 carry sufficient public feedback-domain detail for the composition analysis, 31 support
the technique–vulnerability cross-tabulation, and 22 are for the detailed head-to-head
comparison; each subset membership is flagged per record in the released dataset.

2.3. Related Works
The literature on smart contract analysis has grown substantially over the past decade,
with numerous surveys covering different facets of the problem. We organise the most
relevant prior work into three groups: (i) smart contract security and vulnerability surveys,
(ii) smart contract fuzzing surveys, and (iii) classical fuzzing surveys. Table 1 compares the
most relevant surveys with our work along key dimensions.
Smart contract security and vulnerability surveys
Zhu et al. [5] (TSC 2024) summarise 27 types of exploited security vulnerabilities from
539 real-world incidents. Hejazi and Lashkari [6] (JNCA 2025) evaluate 256 smart contract
analysis tools categorised by methodology; their scope is broad and does not provide a
fuzzing architectural taxonomy or technique genealogy. Wei et al. [17] (ACM CSUR 2025)
present a “Survey on Quality Assurance of Smart Contracts” that classifies 40 vulnerability
types; fuzzing occupies a small part without an architecture survey. Dong et al. [18], Qian
et al. [19], and Kushwaha et al. [20] survey vulnerability detection broadly.
Smart contract fuzzing surveys
Wu et al. [21] (ICSE 2024) provide the closest prior work: they compare state-of-the-art
fuzzers by re-running them on a shared benchmark, exposing performance inconsistencies.
Their contribution is primarily empirical; they do not survey architectural components,
classify mutation operators, or provide a research roadmap. Our survey complements
their empirical findings with architectural and taxonomic depth. Andrijasa et al. [22]
survey multi-agent and RL-based fuzzing for smart contracts. Mnasri et al. [23] provide a
comprehensive overview of Ethereum smart contract security testing. Tolmach et al. [7]

3. A Taxonomy of Fuzzing: From Classical to Smart Contract Fuzzing
This section presents the formalisms and concepts used for fuzzing in the literature, and establishes a taxonomy that connects classical fuzzing with the smart contract
fuzzing domain. The classification is organised into two broad categories: classical fuzzing
(Section 3.1), which covers input–feedback models and guidance strategies applicable to

general software, and smart contract fuzzing (Section 3.2), which addresses the domainspecific input space, state model, and oracles required when fuzzing Ethereum or EVMcompatible contracts. This structure mirrors the contract-level vs. program-level distinction
in formal verification surveys [7], and provides the conceptual foundation for the architectural survey (Section 4) and the techniques survey (Section 5). This section directly
supports the answers to RQ1–RQ4.
3.1. Classical Fuzzing: Concepts and Input–Feedback Models
In classical fuzzing, the target program is executed with generated or mutated inputs;
the goal is to trigger bugs, crashes, or violations of desired properties. The following
concepts are central to the taxonomy:
•

•

•

Input space—The set of all possible inputs to the program. For traditional software
this may be byte streams (e.g., file or network input), structured data (e.g., XML,
JSON), or protocol messages. Inputs can be generated from scratch (generation-based)
or produced by mutating existing seeds (mutation-based).
Feedback—Information observed from execution, used to guide the fuzzer. Common
feedback includes: whether the program crashed or hung; code coverage (e.g., branch
or edge coverage); and custom oracles (e.g., assertion failure, sanitiser report). Blackbox fuzzers use no execution feedback; greybox fuzzers use lightweight feedback such
as coverage; and whitebox approaches use full program analysis.
Guidance—The strategy that determines which inputs are retained, prioritised, or
mutated. Guidance may be random, coverage-guided (favour inputs that reach new
code), grammar-based (respect input structure), or property-based (target specific
invariants or violation conditions).

3.1.1. Input Space and Feedback in Classical Fuzzing
Coverage-guided greybox fuzzing (CGF) has become the dominant paradigm for
vulnerability discovery in traditional software. The two most influential tools are AFL
(American Fuzzy Lop) [12], which pioneered compile-time instrumentation and edgecoverage-guided mutation, and libFuzzer [13], which provides in-process, coverage-guided
fuzzing integrated with LLVM sanitisers. Together they have inspired hundreds of descendants and have been adopted by major software projects (e.g., OSS-Fuzz [36], Google’s
continuous fuzzing infrastructure). Honggfuzz [37] adds hardware-based coverage feedback and multi-threaded execution.
AFL internals and power scheduling.
AFL instruments the target binary at compile time (or via QEMU/Unicorn for binaryonly targets) to collect an edge-coverage bitmap: a fixed-size array indexed by the hash
of (source, destination) basic-block pairs. During fuzzing, AFL maintains a corpus of
inputs, each tagged with the set of edges it covers. An input is retained if it covers at
least one new edge. The power schedule determines how many mutations are applied
to each seed: AFL’s default schedule assigns more “energy” (mutation rounds) to seeds
that are smaller, faster to execute, and cover rare edges. Subsequent work (AFLFast [38],
MOpt [39], FairFuzz [40]) refines the power schedule using Markov-chain models, particleswarm optimisation of mutation operator distributions, and fairness-based prioritisation
of rare branches. These scheduling innovations are directly transferable to smart contract
fuzzing, where sFuzz [41] adopts AFL-style power scheduling and MuFuzz [42] introduces
sequence-level energy assignment.

libFuzzer and sanitiser integration.
libFuzzer [13] operates in-process: the target function is compiled as a library and
called repeatedly in the same address space, avoiding fork overhead. It integrates tightly
with LLVM sanitisers (AddressSanitizer, MemorySanitizer, UndefinedBehaviourSanitizer),
which serve as powerful oracles beyond crash detection. This in-process model is analogous
to smart contract fuzzers that embed an EVM emulator (e.g., Echidna [43], ItyFuzz [44])
rather than interacting with a separate blockchain node, achieving much higher throughput.
Domain-specific feedback and waypoints.
Padhye et al. [45] (OOPSLA 2019) introduce FuzzFactory, a classical fuzzing framework
included in our corpus under classical fuzzing tools, for developing domain-specific fuzzing
applications by extending the CGF algorithm with waypoints: intermediate inputs that
make progress toward domain-specific objectives beyond code coverage, without requiring
changes to mutation or search heuristics. Users specify domain-specific feedback collection
and aggregation; the framework then selectively saves waypoints that improve along these
custom dimensions. FuzzFactory demonstrates that six distinct domain-specific fuzzing
applications can be unified under a single framework and, crucially, composed to achieve
emergent capabilities. Concretely, a waypoint is an input saved not because it adds code
coverage but because it makes monotonic progress on a domain-specific feedback map
dsf : K → V (feedback keys K, a partially ordered value set V, and a max/min aggregation
recording the best value per key); an input is retained if it strictly improves the aggregate
for at least one key in some active domain, and domains compose disjunctively (retain-ifwaypoint-in-any). Its six sample applications each add one such domain—targeting, e.g.,
slow paths, input validity, and comparison-progress smoothing of magic-value branches;
composing the validity domain with the comparison-smoothing domain discovers wellformed inputs that also clear hard equality guards, with each domain unblocking the
other—the emergent capability. This waypoint concept is directly relevant to smart contract
fuzzing, where domain-specific feedback such as storage-access patterns (sFuzz2.0 [46]),
state novelty (ItyFuzz [44]), and oracle-specific signals go beyond branch coverage; indeed,
ItyFuzz’s snapshot mechanism can be viewed as an instantiation of the waypoint pattern
for stateful contract exploration (RQ4).
Systematic surveys and open challenges.
Böhme, Cadar, and Roychoudhury [25] reflect on the open problems of fuzzing, including how to fuzz stateful software and software with highly structured inputs. Building on
their reflections and the challenge framing of Torres et al. [47], we organise three challenges
that recur across fuzzing domains and are particularly acute for smart contracts: (i) semantic validity—ensuring that generated inputs are accepted by the program’s parser and
reach deep logic; (ii) stateful exploration—effectively navigating systems whose behaviour
depends on accumulated state; and (iii) environment modelling—faithfully simulating the
program’s operating environment (files, network, hardware). All three challenges manifest
acutely in smart contract fuzzing, where inputs must conform to the ABI format (semantic
validity), execution is inherently stateful (state exploration), and the blockchain environment (gas, block context, other contracts) must be modelled (environment modelling).
Mallissery and Wu [27] provide a comprehensive taxonomy of fuzzing methods—randommutation, feedback-driven, coverage-guided, constraint-guided, seed-scheduling, and
target-oriented—and demonstrate that no single strategy dominates; effectiveness depends
on the combination of method, tool, and target. Their taxonomy informs our technique
classification in Section 5.
Surveys of classical fuzzing [29,31] systematise the input space (e.g., for network
protocol fuzzing, the input is message sequences with a structure defined by the protocol)

3.1.2. Greybox, Grammar-Based, and Property-Based Fuzzing
Beyond pure coverage guidance, classical fuzzing includes grammar-based fuzzing,
where inputs are generated according to a grammar or schema so that they are syntactically valid and more likely to reach deep code; property-based or invariant-based fuzzing
(pioneered by QuickCheck [48]), where the goal is to violate user-defined or mined invariants; and symbolic or concolic hybrids, where symbolic execution (KLEE [49], SAGE [50])
helps to solve path constraints to generate inputs that reach specific branches. Driller [51]
demonstrates the power of selectively combining coverage-guided fuzzing with concolic
execution: when the fuzzer stalls, Driller invokes symbolic execution on difficult branches
and feeds solutions back as seeds—a pattern adopted by smart contract hybrid fuzzers (ConFuzzius [47], TPH-Fuzz [52], WASAIUP [53]). Protocol fuzzing [31] and kernel fuzzing [32]
surveys emphasise the importance of input structure and of feedback that goes beyond
raw coverage (e.g., state machine coverage, protocol semantics). These techniques are
transferable in principle to smart contract fuzzing (RQ4), where the “input” is a sequence
of transactions and the “grammar” is the ABI and transaction format.
Following Manès et al. [24], a classical fuzzing campaign repeatedly selects a seed from
a corpus, generates a mutated input, executes the target, extracts a feedback signal (typically
an edge-coverage bitmap), and updates the corpus if the signal is novel. Grammar-based
fuzzing constrains the mutator to produce structurally valid inputs; property-based fuzzing
replaces the crash oracle with a user-defined predicate. The waypoint generalisation of this
corpus-update rule (FuzzFactory [45]) underpins the unified architecture we develop in
Section 4.2.
3.2. Smart Contract Fuzzing: Domain and Input Models
Smart contracts execute on a blockchain in a global, persistent state. Their behaviour
is triggered by transactions: each transaction specifies a callee (contract address), a function
selector and arguments (the calldata), and an optional value transfer. The state (storage,
balances, and the effect of previous transactions) evolves with each execution. Thus, the
input space for smart contract fuzzing is not a single byte stream but sequences of transactions together with the initial or evolving state. The key difference from classical fuzzing
follows directly: rather than mutating a byte input to a stateless function, a smart contract
fuzzer must synthesise ABI-conformant transaction sequences that drive this persistent
state and judge the outcome with a domain-specific oracle over the whole execution trace

(transactions must conform to the ABI and satisfy type/value constraints), stateful exploration (execution is inherently stateful, with each transaction modifying persistent storage),
and environment modelling (the blockchain environment—gas model, block context, other
deployed contracts—must be faithfully simulated). Each of these challenges has an analogue in classical fuzzing—grammar-based fuzzing for structured inputs, protocol and
stateful fuzzing for accumulated state, and property-based fuzzing for non-crash oracles
(Section 3.1.2)—but what is specific to smart contracts is that all three arise together, and by
construction, in every campaign. These characteristics have profound implications for how
fuzzing techniques from classical software must be adapted. We first introduce a formal
model of the smart contract fuzzing problem (Section 3.2.1) and then discuss how it is

3.2.1. Smart Contract Fuzzing Model
Smart contract fuzzing extends classical fuzzing along three dimensions (Table 3):
(i) the input is a transaction sequence rather than a byte stream, where each transaction
specifies a callee, function selector, arguments, Ether value, and environmental context;
(ii) the execution is stateful—each transaction modifies persistent storage and balances, so
the order and content of the sequence jointly determine the outcome; and (iii) the oracle is
domain-specific and must inspect the full execution trace across the sequence, not merely
a single crash or assertion failure. Coverage feedback in the smart contract setting can
be gathered at branch level (used by most CGF tools), at storage-access level (tracking
read/write patterns over contract storage slots, as introduced by sFuzz2.0 [46]) or at state
level, recording the set of distinct blockchain states reached, as in ItyFuzz’s snapshot
mechanism [44]. In a typical coverage-guided smart contract fuzzing loop, seeds are ABIinitialised, selected by a power schedule, mutated at the sequence level (by reordering,
inserting, or splicing transactions), executed in an embedded EVM, and retained if they
cover new code or trigger an oracle violation. Hybrid tools additionally invoke an SMT
solver on unreached branches to generate targeted seeds, then resume greybox fuzzing
with the enriched corpus.
Assessing how well such a fuzzer tests a contract requires more than code coverage.
Jain et al. [54] show that code coverage and mutation score, the fraction of injected code
mutants a test suite kills, are only weakly correlated: coverage shows that a line of code was
executed, whereas killing a mutant on that line shows that the suite’s oracles can actually
distinguish faulty behaviour. A campaign can therefore reach near-total coverage yet still
miss injected faults. A second, smart contract-specific concern is oracle coverage, how
many vulnerability classes the fuzzer’s detectors actually check, since a fuzzer that never
instantiates, say, a reentrancy oracle cannot find reentrancy bugs regardless of coverage.
Most tools nonetheless report coverage alone.

3.2.2. Transaction and State Models in Practice
At the contract level, smart contract fuzzing can be viewed as generating and mutating transaction sequences. Each element of the sequence is a transaction (or a block of
transactions); the state after each transaction becomes the context for the next. Wu et al. [21]
provide a critical analysis of state-of-the-art smart contract fuzzers and emphasise the need
to explore stateful behaviours, multi-transaction sequences, and cross-contract interactions.
Tools such as sFuzz [41], ConFuzz [55], and Midas [56] operate over transaction sequences
and use coverage or custom oracles to guide exploration. Erinys [57] focuses on function
invoke sequence generation; MuFuzz [42] introduces sequence-aware mutation and seed
mask guidance; and CrossFuzz [58] and xFuzz target cross-contract vulnerability detection.
3.2.3. Oracles and Vulnerability Targets
Smart contract vulnerabilities are well-catalogued. Zhu et al. [5] summarise 27 types
of exploited security vulnerabilities from 539 real-world incidents on Ethereum; Hejazi and
Lashkari [6] evaluate 256 analysis tools including fuzzers. Complementary tools (Oyente [9],
Mythril [10], Slither [8]) form the broader ecosystem and many hybrid fuzzers integrate with
them. The common vulnerability classes are cross-tabulated against technique categories
and representative fuzzers in the Discussion.
Three principal oracle designs cover the major vulnerability classes. A reentrancy
oracle detects that a contract appears in the call stack while an external call from that
same contract is still pending—including read-only reentrancy [59], where the reentrant
call reads a stale state and induces incorrect downstream behaviour. An access-control
oracle flags when a protected function is successfully invoked by an unauthorised sender
(no revert). A financial property oracle, as in FinanceFuzz [60], checks invariant and
equivalence properties such as token-supply conservation and balance equivalence. Most
fuzzers combine multiple oracles with a disjunctive strategy (fire if any oracle triggers),
which maximises recall at the cost of precision; the high false-positive rates reported in
independent evaluations [21] are plausibly associated with this design. The technique-vsvulnerability breakdown (Table 7) shows that reentrancy and arithmetic are targeted across
all fuzzing-oriented categories, while financial properties and front-running are addressed
by fewer than 10% of tools.
3.3. Summary
Section 3 has introduced a taxonomy linking classical fuzzing (input space, feedback,
guidance) with smart contract fuzzing (transaction and state models, oracles, vulnerability
targets). Table 3 makes the correspondence precise. The three challenges of semantic validity, stateful exploration, and environment modelling [25,47] manifest directly in the smart
contract setting, and the multi-strategy perspective of Mallissery and Wu [27] motivates the
technique category organisation of Section 5. The taxonomy clarifies how general limitations (RQ1) and smart contract-specific limitations (RQ2, RQ3) arise, and which techniques
are transferable or underexplored (RQ4).

how they accomplish it: Section 4 surveys the architectural building blocks (execution
engines, feedback mechanisms, scheduling strategies) that translate these concepts into
working systems.

4.1.1. Input Representation and Generation
The input space for a smart contract fuzzer is the set of possible inputs that can be
fed to the execution layer. In the smart contract setting, this is typically a transaction
sequence: an ordered list of transactions, each specifying a callee (contract address), a
function selector (or calldata), arguments, and an optional value. Some fuzzers also control
the initial state (e.g., account balances, storage) or environment (block number, timestamp).
The generation or mutation component produces or modifies these inputs. Generation may
be random, grammar-based (using the contract ABI or a schema), or learning-based (e.g.,
LLM-generated sequences [61], reinforcement learning [62]). Mutation operates on existing
seeds: it may change the order of transactions, the arguments or value of a call, or the
length of the sequence. Tools such as sFuzz [41] and sFuzz2.0 [46] use coverage to guide
which seeds to mutate; MuFuzz [42] introduces sequence-aware mutation and seed mask
guidance; and ConFuzz [55] targets large-scale fuzz testing by scaling the input generation
and execution pipeline.

4.1.2. Execution Engine Design
The execution layer runs the contract (or a model of it) on a given input. A critical
architectural choice is how the execution engine is implemented; we identify three principal
designs used across the surveyed tools:
•

•

•

Fork-based (external node): The fuzzer communicates with a separate Ethereum
node (e.g., Ganache, Hardhat Network, a private geth instance) via JSON-RPC. Each
transaction is executed by this local node exactly as it would be on-chain, but on a
private chain, so no mainnet gas or Ether is spent, and the node manages the state.
ContractFuzzer [63] uses this approach, running against a private Ethereum network.
Advantage: Highest fidelity (the full EVM semantics, including gas metering and
precompiled contracts, are faithfully reproduced). Disadvantage: The IPC overhead of
JSON-RPC limits throughput to hundreds of transactions per second, which is orders
of magnitude slower than in-process execution.
In-process (embedded EVM): The fuzzer embeds an EVM implementation as a library
(e.g., Echidna [43] uses Hevm, a Haskell EVM implementation; ItyFuzz [44] uses a
custom Rust-based EVM). Transaction execution occurs in the same process as the
fuzzer, eliminating IPC overhead and enabling throughput of thousands to tens of
thousands of transactions per second. This is analogous to libFuzzer’s [13] in-process
model for classical fuzzing (Section 3.1.1). Advantage: High throughput, direct
access to execution traces, and easy instrumentation. Disadvantage: The embedded
EVM may not implement all opcodes or precompiled contracts, introducing potential
fidelity gaps.
Snapshot-based: ItyFuzz [44] introduced snapshot management: the EVM state is periodically checkpointed, and the fuzzer can fork from any snapshot without replaying
the entire transaction history. This avoids the cost of state reconstruction and enables
efficient exploration of the state space from multiple starting points. sFuzz2.0 [46] uses
a similar mechanism for storage-access tracking. Advantage: Enables efficient stateful
exploration without full replay. Disadvantage: Snapshot management introduces
memory overhead.

The choice of execution engine has a direct impact on the fuzzer’s throughput, fidelity,
and the types of vulnerabilities it can detect (e.g., gas-related bugs require accurate gas
metering, which fork-based engines provide but simplified embedded EVMs may not).
Modern tools increasingly favour in-process execution with snapshot support as the best
compromise between throughput and fidelity.
4.1.3. Feedback and Scheduling
The feedback mechanism observes the execution and extracts the signals used to
guide the fuzzer. Common feedback includes: (i) code coverage (e.g., branch or edge
coverage, often obtained by instrumenting the EVM or bytecode); (ii) crash or revert;
and (iii) custom oracles (e.g., reentrancy detection, gas anomalies, balance conservation).
Coverage instrumentation can be performed at the source level (Solidity, before compilation), at the bytecode level (inserting probe instructions into EVM bytecode), or within
the EVM interpreter itself (modifying the execution loop to record branch hits). Sourcelevel instrumentation provides the highest granularity but requires access to source code;
bytecode-level instrumentation (used by sFuzz [41] and SMARTIAN [64]) works on deployed contracts but may miss compiler-inserted code; EVM-level instrumentation (used
by ItyFuzz) captures all executed code including library and proxy code. The scheduling or
prioritisation component uses this feedback to decide which inputs to retain in the corpus
and which to mutate next. Many fuzzers integrate with static analysis (e.g., CSAFuzzer [65]
combines fuzzing with static analysis; SMARTIAN [64] uses static and dynamic data-flow

analyses) or with symbolic execution (e.g., ConFuzzius [47], TPH-Fuzz [52], WASAIUP [53]).
Data flow from the input space through the execution layer to the feedback component,
and feedback drives both generation (or mutation) and scheduling.

4.2. A Generalised Waypoint Architecture for Smart Contract Fuzzing
We observe that the architectural diversity among smart contract fuzzers can be unified
under a single generalised framework. Padhye et al. [45] introduced FuzzFactory, which
extends coverage-guided fuzzing with domain-specific feedback (DSF) and waypoints:
inputs saved not because they improve code coverage, but because they make monotonic
progress along a user-defined, domain-specific dimension. In this subsection, we use
this as an open, extensible lens under which the smart contract fuzzers we survey can
be described as instantiations of a generalised waypoint architecture, differing in which
feedback domains they define and how they compose them. The eight domains are those we
could ground in FuzzFactory’s DSF model and observe in the corpus, not a necessary-andsufficient closed set; further domains—e.g., call-context novelty, protocol-level economic
state, inter-contract topology, temporal properties, or environment feedback—may exist and
can be added within the same framework. This unification reveals precisely which feedback
dimensions are well-explored, which are missing, and how tools relate to one another.
4.2.1. Waypoint Domains for Smart Contracts
Under the FuzzFactory framework, each feedback dimension is specified as a domain:
a set of feedback keys (e.g., branch IDs, storage slots, state hashes), a set of observed values,
and an aggregation operator (typically max or min). A new transaction sequence is saved
as a waypoint if it makes strict progress (i.e., changes the aggregate) for at least one key
in at least one active domain. Domains compose via disjunction: a sequence is retained
if it is a waypoint in any constituent domain. The domains are composable dimensions
rather than a partition: they are neither mutually exclusive nor hierarchical, and a tool may
instantiate several at once. This design guarantees that adding domains can only increase
the set of retained sequences, never decrease it, and that progress is monotonic within
each domain independently. This is a set-theoretic property of the disjunctive definition,
not a guarantee of improved detection in practice, where finite time and memory, seed
prioritisation, and scheduling overhead can offset it. For smart contracts, feedback hooks
intercept EVM opcodes (JUMPI for branches, SLOAD/SSTORE for storage, CALL for intercontract interaction) rather than LLVM IR constructs, which is the only adaptation required
to apply FuzzFactory’s framework to this setting.

4.2.3. Tool-Level Domain Composition
We analysed the domain composition of all 33 representative fuzzers. Among the
29 with sufficient public domain information (Olympia, Harvey, GasFuzzer, and Fuse are
excluded as they either serve as benchmarks or lack public feedback-domain detail), only a
handful of tools compose as many as three domains (e.g., ItyFuzz: branch coverage, data
flow, and state novelty); most use only two (dbr + doracle ); and none approach the full eight.
The released Feedback_Coverage field lets any reader re-derive this coding.
4.2.4. Gap Analysis: Missing Domain Compositions
Several structural gaps are evident: (1) storage access (dst , used by only a few tools) is
not combined with state novelty (dσ ), which would give deeper stateful coverage; (2) property violation (dprop ) is rarely combined with state-novelty feedback; (3) financial oracles
lack directed target-distance feedback; and (4) no tool treats cross-contract reachability as a
first-class waypoint domain distinct from constraint solving. More broadly, composition
beyond two domains is rare, despite FuzzFactory showing that even two-domain composition yields emergent capabilities [45]. These constitute research directions (RQ4) require no
change to the underlying mutation engine.

5.1. Coverage-Guided (Greybox) Fuzzing
Coverage-guided fuzzing (CGF), the dominant paradigm (51.2% of tools), instruments
the EVM to collect branch (or edge) coverage and guides mutation toward underexplored
code. The line of work opened with ContractFuzzer [63] (ASE 2018), among the earliest SC
fuzzers, which combined ABI-based generation with seven bug oracles. Throughput and
scheduling improved rapidly thereafter; sFuzz [41] (ICSE 2020) added AFL-style power
scheduling, achieving broader branch coverage than ContractFuzzer at roughly two orders
of magnitude higher throughput, while Echidna [43] (ISSTA 2020) introduced propertybased CGF with user-defined Solidity invariants and is widely used in practice. Later tools
target specific weaknesses of pure coverage guidance, Harvey [67] (ESEC/FSE 2020) uses
input prediction to bypass hard branches. Sequence-level variants include MuFuzz [42]
(seed-mask mutation), MagicMirror [73] (combinatorial testing), and ConFuzz [55] (largescale parallel fuzzing). Directed CGF tools (LDfuzz [74], Vulseye [80]) steer exploration
via distance metrics. sFuzz2.0 [46] extends branch coverage with storage-access-pattern
feedback, reporting 20.49% more detected vulnerabilities and 37.53% more branch coverage
than sFuzz. Wu et al. [21] show that no single CGF tool dominates across benchmarks;
sFuzz and Echidna are the standard baselines. A key challenge is that coverage plateaus
quickly, and the remaining code often requires specific argument values or long transaction
sequences that only hybrid or directed approaches can reach.
5.2. Property-Based and Invariant Fuzzing
Property-based fuzzing (PBF) (3.3% of tools) aims to violate user-defined invariants
rather than merely trigger crashes. Echidna [43] is the canonical example: users write
echidna_* boolean predicates and Echidna searches for a transaction sequence that falsifies
one. FinanceFuzz [60] extends the idea of DeFi-specific invariant and equivalence properties
such as token-supply conservation and balance equivalence across equivalent transaction
sequences. Invariants can be user-supplied (Echidna, Foundry), mined from execution
traces, or generated automatically. SmartACE [84] bridges PBF with bounded model
checking for combined “fuzz-then-verify” workflows. The main barrier to adoption is the
specification effort, which LLM-assisted invariant generation may help to lower.
5.3. Symbolic and Concolic Fuzzing
Hybrid symbolic and concolic fuzzers (24.0%) combine concrete execution with symbolic constraint solving to reach branches that random mutation cannot. ConFuzzius [47]
(EuroS&P 2021) uses demand-driven SMT solving (Z3) to pass hard branches, invoking
the solver only when greybox progress stalls. SMARTIAN [64] (ASE 2021) adds static and
dynamic data-flow analysis to order transactions and guide mutation. Although greybox
rather than concolic, ItyFuzz [44] (ISSTA 2023) is often discussed alongside hybrid tools
for its snapshot-based state management (O(1) state restoration vs. O(k) replay), which
enables efficient exploration of stateful contracts that prior tools could not reach. Later
designs focus on controlling solver costs. TPH-Fuzz [52] uses a two-phase design: broad
CGF then targeted symbolic. WASAIUP [53] brings demand-driven concolic fuzzing to
EOSIO/Wasm contracts. Ding et al. [85] extend hybrid fuzzing to DeFi protocol-level
vulnerabilities via context-sensitive concolic verification. The main limitations are inherited
from symbolic execution: path explosion, intractable cryptographic constraints and high
cost for long transaction sequences.
5.4. Learning-Based Fuzzing (ML, RL, LLM)
Learning-based fuzzers (13 of 121, 10.7%) use a learned model within the fuzzing
loop as the seed/mutation generator, the transaction-selection policy, or the vulnerability

https://doi.org/10.3390/app16189106

oracle applied to executed inputs. They comprise three model classes: classical machine
learning (7), reinforcement learning (3), and large language models (3), released per-tool in
the dataset’s learning_subclass/learning_mechanism fields. Static ML/DL classifiers that
label a contract without generating or executing inputs are excluded from the core under
EC2. Among LLM-guided tools, Sun et al. [61] (Autom. Softw. Eng. 2024) use chain-based
LLMs for adversarial seed generation, reporting an average instruction-coverage gain of
≈3% over a single-step LLM baseline, and CrossGuard [82] queries an LLM at runtime
for cross-contract mutation hints; among reinforcement-learning tools, Su et al. [62] model
fuzzing as a Markov decision process to learn a transaction-selection policy. Reproducibility
in this category depends on whether the underlying model is open or closed: closed- or APIserved models are exposed to silent model drift and prompt sensitivity, so a campaign may
not be re-runnable even when its code is released; Bamohabbat Chafjiri et al. [28] likewise
identify training cost and data dependency as barriers to adoption. Within the learningbased category, LLM-guided fuzzing is the newest and fastest-emerging subcategory
(predominantly 2024 onward), by recency rather than by count.
5.5. Mutation and Seed Generation Strategies
Across surveyed tools, mutation is built from six primitive mutation operators: argument mutation, function mutation, insertion/deletion, reorder, splice, and environment mutation (sender, timestamp, gas). Most tools apply ABI-aware random or coverage-directed
variants of these; Cao et al. [86] find that the choice and stacking depth of Havoc-style mutators strongly affect coverage gains. Which mutations receive the most effort is governed
by a power schedule (AFL-style energy assignment), adapted by MuFuzz and Vulseye but
still underexplored at the sequence level. Beyond random and coverage-directed mutation,
metaheuristic search has also been applied: EvoFuzzer [78] uses a genetic algorithm to
evolve transaction sequences for reentrancy detection, and ACOFuzz [83] uses ant colony
optimisation over the control-flow graph to guide exploration. Seed generation, finally,
ranges from ABI/grammar-based (sFuzz, Echidna) to static-analysis-guided (DepFuzz [81],
Erinys [57]), on-chain replay (ItyFuzz, Midas), and learning-based (LLM seeds [61]).

5.6. Differential and Platform-Specific Fuzzing
Several further directions broaden smart contract fuzzing beyond single-contract vulnerability discovery. Differential fuzzers compare implementations and flag divergent
behaviour as a bug: EVMFuzzer [87] across EVM implementations, Tian et al. [88] across
Solidity compiler versions, and Fluffy and LOKI [89,90] across blockchain-client implementations. Platform-specific fuzzers port these techniques beyond Ethereum: EOSFuzzer [70]
and WASAIUP [53] target EOSIO, Fuzz on the Beach [91] targets Solana, HFContractFuzzer [92] targets Hyperledger Fabric, and Hochrainer et al. [93] target zero-knowledge
circuits. Cross-contract fuzzers such as CrossFuzz [58], xFuzz [76], and Pluto [71] reason
about inter-contract interactions. Finally, exploit-generation tools synthesise working attacks rather than merely flagging vulnerabilities: EthPloit [69] and EF CF [94] generate
exploits directly, and Verite [95] extracts over $18M across 61 real-world DeFi exploits
through profit-directed mutation.
5.7. Technique Comparison
The categories occupy distinct points in the effectiveness–cost trade-off. Coverageguided fuzzing offers the highest throughput and the simplest deployment, but its coverage
plateaus on branches guarded by specific values or reachable only through long transaction
sequences. Property-based fuzzing provides the strongest, semantically meaningful oracles
(user invariants), at the cost of manual specification effort. Hybrid symbolic/concolic
fuzzing reaches guard-protected branches that pure mutation cannot, but inherits symbolic
execution’s path explosion and solver cost. Learning-based fuzzing can improve seed
quality and mutation/policy guidance, but its reproducibility depends on whether the
underlying model is open or closed, and it is the least mature category. Differential
and platform-specific fuzzing extend testing to cross-implementation and non-Ethereum
targets, but require multiple reference implementations or platform-specific harnesses. No
single category dominates across benchmarks [21], which is why tool portfolios combining
coverage-guided, hybrid, and property-based fuzzers are the most effective practical
strategy (Section 6.3). Table 6 draws these categories together, comparing 22 representative
tools along five axes: input model, coverage type, oracle, hybrid or ML integration, and
key innovation. Two trends stand out. First, almost all tools now operate on transaction
sequences rather than single transactions, reflecting the stateful nature of contract execution.
Second, most rely on plain branch coverage together with a reentrancy and arithmeticoriented oracle suite; richer feedback (edge, storage-access, or property-level coverage)
and hybrid or ML integration remain the exception rather than the rule, mirroring the
composition gap identified in Section 4.2.4.

6.2. Answers to Research Questions
RQ1. Classical limitations (coverage bottlenecks, oracle design beyond crashes, scalability, ML cost) carry over to SC fuzzing and are amplified. The three challenges of
semantic validity, stateful exploration, and environment modelling [25,47] all manifest
simultaneously in the SC setting.
RQ2. The input is a transaction sequence (not a byte stream); execution is stateful; and oracles must inspect multi-transaction traces. These three structural differences help account for the shift from random to coverage-guided to hybrid approaches.
Jain et al. [54] confirm that branch coverage is insufficient: high coverage does not imply
high mutation score.
RQ3. EVM-specific constraints (256-bit words, gas metering, DELEGATECALL proxy patterns, ABI encoding, and compiler-version sensitivity) help account for the 83.5% Ethereum
concentration. Tools requiring source code (Echidna, Foundry) cannot analyse bytecodeonly contracts; DeepInfer [96] partially bridges this via deep learning-type recovery.
RQ4. Feedback-domain composition is sparse—the large majority of tools compose at
most two of the eight domains and none approach the full eight (Section 4.2.4)—leaving several high-value multi-domain compositions unexplored. Property-based testing, grammarbased fuzzing, and dictionary mutation are underexplored (<5% each). SC innovations
(stateful directed fuzzing, domain-specific oracles) can transfer to classical fuzzing of
stateful systems [33].
6.3. Evaluation Methodology and Real-World Impact
No universally adopted benchmark exists: evaluation corpora range from handcollected contract sets and the SmartBugs dataset to real-world DeFi protocols, and
Wu et al. [21] demonstrate that the same fuzzer can appear effective or ineffective depending on which is chosen. Statistical rigour is also limited: significance testing is only
rarely reported. Five recurring anti-patterns undermine comparability: (1) tuned-vs-default
comparison; (2) cherry-picked contracts; (3) coverage-only evaluation; (4) missing baselines
from the same technique category; and (5) single-run results without statistical analysis [97].
These five are recurring patterns illustrated by examples, identified during full-text reading,
not an exhaustive per-paper census, and we do not use them to weight the self-reported results of individual papers. Reproducibility is uneven: while a number of tools release their
source code or a public repository, many are closed-source or do not disclose availability
at all (the per-tool status is released in the Availability field of the accompanying dataset).
More importantly, the finer artefacts that genuine re-execution requires—an exact commit
or version, build instructions, a pinned dependency specification, the seeds and fuzzing
configurations used, the evaluation hardware, and an archivally persistent deposit—are
essentially never reported in the surveyed papers, so their systematic absence, rather than

https://doi.org/10.3390/app16189106

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 9106

25 of 32

tool-by-tool variation, is the reproducibility finding. Even among the open-source tools,
a reader typically cannot recover the exact configuration that produced the published
numbers, which is why independent studies repeatedly re-implement baselines rather than
reuse published artefacts.
In practice, Echidna [43] is a widely used property-based SC fuzzer, ItyFuzz adds onchain state loading for triaging deployed contracts, and Foundry’s Forge integrates fuzzing
into the Solidity development workflow. Independent evaluations [6,21] consistently
find that no single tool dominates: hybrid tools achieve the broadest vulnerability-class
coverage, while CGF tools achieve the highest throughput. In practice, therefore, fuzzer
portfolios running Echidna, ItyFuzz, and SMARTIAN in parallel emerge as the most
practical deployment strategy.

6.5. Threats to Validity
Several threats to validity should be noted, together with the steps taken to limit them.
Search completeness is an external threat: to reduce the risk of missing relevant work, we
queried two complementary databases (Scopus and Google Scholar) with different indexing
scopes and screened all records following the PRISMA 2020 protocol. We nonetheless
queried only these two indices, so works indexed in neither may have been missed, and
because the Google Scholar component relies on the allintitle operator, which matches title
terms only and runs against a non-deterministic, localisation-sensitive ranking, in-scope
works whose titles omit our query terms may have been under-retrieved and exact result
sets may not be reproducible. We mitigate the latter by releasing the raw Scholar export
(all_scholar.csv), so the retained records remain verifiable even where the live query is not.
Classification subjectivity is an internal threat, which we mitigated by applying a fixed
coding scheme with explicit category definitions and cross-checking each classification
against the tool’s primary source. Several further threats we note explicitly. The corpus is

https://doi.org/10.3390/app16189106

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 9106

26 of 32

restricted to works with a full text in English (EC3), which may omit relevant non-English
studies. The literature is subject to publication bias toward positive tool evaluations and
to incremental republication of the same system across venues, and our headline figures
rely on authors’ self-reported results, which we do not independently re-run. We apply
no formal per-paper study-quality weighting: quality is treated only as context through
the venue tier, evaluation anti-patterns, and open-source availability. The 33 representative
tools are chosen by purposive maximum-variation sampling and are therefore not a random
sample. Distinguishing a tool from the paper that describes it is occasionally ambiguous
for multi-paper systems, which we resolve by collapsing duplicate editions in the released
dataset. The corpus is also strongly EVM/Ethereum-oriented, which we report as an
empirical property of the field rather than a scope restriction, and findings on LLM-guided
fuzzing may date quickly given the pace of that subfield. Finally, because indexing lags
behind research activity, papers appearing close to our final search date of 15 February 2026
may be under-represented, a cut-off we state explicitly so that the coverage of the survey
can be interpreted accordingly.

7. Conclusions
This survey reviewed the recent advances in smart contract fuzzing with a focus on
methods, techniques, and architectures, organised around four research questions that
link classical and smart contract-specific fuzzing. We synthesise our findings across the
four research questions (answered in detail in the Discussion, Section 6) and close with
an outlook.
The general fuzzing limitations of RQ1 all carry over to, and are amplified in, the
smart contract setting. They comprise coverage bottlenecks, oracle design beyond crash
detection, scalability, and the cost and interpretability of ML/LLM guidance, together with
the challenges of semantic validity, stateful exploration, and environment modelling [25,47].
Here, the stateless execution model, byte-level input space, and crash-centric oracle of
classical fuzzing must each be generalised (Table 3). Two further factors shape the landscape.
At the domain level (RQ2), smart contracts differ from classical targets in three main
ways: their inputs are transaction sequences, their execution is stateful, and their oracles
must inspect execution traces. At the platform level (RQ3), EVM-specific constraints add
further demands, including 256-bit words, gas metering, DELEGATECALL proxy patterns, ABI
encoding, and compiler-version sensitivity. Together, these factors are consistent with the
prevalence of coverage-guided greybox fuzzing (51.2% of the 121), the 83.5% concentration
on Ethereum/EVM targets, and the under-service of cross-contract and financial (<10%)
vulnerability classes. For transferability (RQ4), generalised waypoint mapping (Table 5,
Section 4.2.4) gives the most concrete result: feedback-domain composition is sparse—the
large majority of smart contract fuzzers compose at most two of the eight domains and
none approach the full eight—leaving several high-value multi-domain compositions
unexplored, since adding domains can only enlarge the retained set, never shrink it [45] (a
formal property, not a demonstrated detection gain). Grammar-based and property-based
fuzzing, dictionary and structure-aware mutation, and LLM/RL-guided generation are
mature in classical fuzzing but still evolving for smart contracts, whereas transactionsequence modelling, stateful directed fuzzing, and domain-specific oracles can flow back
to classical fuzzing of stateful and protocol-oriented systems [31,33].
Beyond the per-RQ findings, our principal contributions fall into three groups. The first
is a set of conceptual artefacts: a classical-to-smart contract fuzzing taxonomy (Table 3), a
technique–vulnerability cross-tabulation (Table 7), and a generalised waypoint architecture
(Table 5) that maps all 33 representative fuzzers to eight composable feedback domains
and identifies several structural composition gaps (Section 4.2.4). The second is a set

https://doi.org/10.3390/app16189106

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 9106

27 of 32

of descriptive analyses of the field: classification and comparison tables (Tables 4–6), a
technique genealogy (Figure 4), bibliometric analyses (Figures 5–7), and an evaluationmethodology analysis of five recurring anti-patterns. The third is a forward-looking
research agenda (Section 7).

Standardisation—shared benchmarks with ground-truth labels, multi-dimensional
adequacy metrics, and mandatory artefact release;
Integration—tighter coupling of fuzzing with formal verification [7], specification
mining, and LLM-assisted invariant generation, extending from contract-level to
protocol-level DeFi properties [85];
Generalisation—extending smart contract fuzzing techniques to cross-chain protocols,
layer-2 rollups, zero-knowledge circuits, and non-Ethereum platforms.

We highlight that smart contract fuzzing, with its inherently stateful execution model
and the economic imperative of protecting over $100 billion in DeFi TVL, offers both a
critical application domain and a rich source of innovations that can advance the broader
fuzzing field.
